Almost every idea can sound promising when described well. The challenge is figuring out which ideas are worth investing time, effort, and resources into.

There is no single formula that guarantees success. But a few questions can help distinguish an interesting concept from an opportunity worth exploring.

## Does it solve a real problem?

A strong idea starts with a reason to exist. It might address a problem people already face, serve an overlooked audience, or offer a meaningfully better way to do something.

The problem does not have to be enormous. What matters is whether the people affected genuinely care about it and whether the proposed solution could make a difference.

## Is there a reason it should exist?

An idea does not need to be completely unprecedented to be valuable. It does, however, need a clear reason to be chosen over existing alternatives.

That reason might be simplicity, accessibility, a better experience, a different audience, or an approach that existing options have overlooked. Understanding that distinction helps turn a broad idea into a clearer proposition.

## Can it be executed?

Ambition matters, but an idea also needs a realistic path forward. What skills are required? What would an initial version look like? Which assumptions need to be tested first? What resources would be necessary?

The answers may reveal that the idea needs to start smaller, find different collaborators, or take a different route. That is not failure; it is part of evaluating the opportunity honestly.

## Could it become sustainable?

Not every worthwhile project needs to become a large company. Still, an idea should have a credible way to continue if it requires ongoing time, money, or operational effort.

Sustainability might come from revenue, a workable operating model, a clear scope, or another realistic way to maintain the work. The right answer depends on the nature of the venture.

## Does it align with the bigger picture?

For an organization supporting multiple ventures, potential alone is not the only consideration. The idea should also fit the organization's values, direction, and ability to contribute meaningfully.

At Nx Conglo, we want to consider ideas on their own merits: what they aim to do, what they need, what potential they demonstrate, and whether there is a sensible basis for pursuing them together.

That does not mean every promising proposal will be accepted. Honest evaluation protects the idea as much as the organization.

## From idea to evidence

A compelling pitch can start a conversation, but progress comes from testing assumptions and learning what works. Speak to the people the idea is meant to help. Build a small version if possible. Look for evidence, not just enthusiasm.

Potential is a reason to investigate an idea. It is not, by itself, proof that the idea will succeed.
